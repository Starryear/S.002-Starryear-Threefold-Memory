# Source Analysis

Complete this analysis internally before prompting or generating. The uploaded photograph is the only visual evidence.

## 1. Identify the subject or core space

Determine:

- the primary subject, or the strongest spatial structure when no single subject exists;
- subject count, category, identity, location, orientation, scale, posture, and action;
- relationships among subjects: size, depth, distance, gaze, interaction, grouping, and separation;
- foreground obstruction, middle ground, background, and environmental boundaries;
- the subject's irreplaceable features.

Use a deletion test:

- Which details can disappear while the subject remains identifiable?
- Which details, if removed, destroy identity or the scene's specific character?

Possible identity anchors vary by source:

- people: facial contour, hairstyle, clothing, gesture, body direction, inter-person distance, distinctive object;
- animals: head shape, ears, horns, wings, tail, proportions, markings, motion;
- plants: petal count, center, branch direction, leaf shape, density, growth rhythm;
- architecture: roof, openings, arches, columns, skyline, perspective, repetition, environment ratio;
- landscapes: landform contour, shoreline, horizon, cloud layer, road, light band, tree distribution, sky-to-ground ratio;
- objects and machines: outer contour, opening, joint, handle, wheel, repeated component, material break, reflection.

These are diagnostic examples, never a required motif list. Rejudge every photograph.

## 2. Extract three to seven visual genes

Choose only the strongest genes. A gene may belong to more than one class.

### Point genes

Real lights, flowers, fruit, windows, spots, stones, drops, distant people, repeated ornament, or highlight particles. They may become rhythm, a path, density change, depth sequence, or time trace only when present in the source.

### Line genes

Body contour, branch, road, river, wire, roof edge, horizon, gaze, fold, shadow boundary, or movement direction. They may be simplified, extended, interrupted, repeated, or reconnected.

### Mass genes

Body, landform, building, canopy, rock, cloud, large shadow, water, clothing, or wall. They may compress into planes or expand into spatial regions.

### Contour genes

The most distinctive outer boundaries. Preserve recognizability without retaining every internal detail.

### Rhythm genes

Repetition, density, height, depth, gathering, dispersal, size change, interval change, or directional change.

### Color genes

Extract the actual deepest tone, lightest tone, subject color, environment color, shadow color, highlight, and smallest accent. Do not substitute a familiar art palette.

For every chosen gene, note:

- exact location in the source;
- role in identity or space;
- whether it should remain intact in the upper panel;
- whether it can migrate, scale, fragment, or change spatial ownership in the lower panel.

## 3. Map the spatial skeleton

Identify:

- visual center and weight;
- subject placement;
- foreground, middle ground, and background;
- horizon and major vertical, horizontal, and diagonal axes;
- overlap and framing;
- negative space;
- light direction;
- visual movement direction.

Both abstract panels may reorganize this skeleton, but their logic must remain traceable to it.

## 4. Multiple subjects

Preserve subject count and the meaningful relations among them: size, front-back order, distance, orientation, interaction, and grouping. The upper may aggregate them into a collective form without silently deleting an important subject. The lower may send them into different visual paths, but the original relationship must remain discoverable. Never add subjects or automatically collapse a group into one figure.

## 5. No single subject

For landscape, street, interior, fog, or abstract light, do not invent a person-like protagonist. Treat the strongest spatial structure, value relation, direction, contour, repetition, horizon, light, color division, or negative space as the subject.

The upper aggregates the scene's structural core. The lower disperses the scene's spatial relationships.

## 6. Mandatory pre-generation questions

Answer internally:

1. What is the true subject or core space?
2. What are the three to seven irreplaceable visual genes?
3. Which features carry identity?
4. Which elements carry spatial structure?
5. Which elements should remain whole?
6. Which can compress into point, line, plane, contour, or rhythm?
7. Which can fragment and migrate in the lower panel?
8. How will the upper aggregate rather than filter?
9. How will the lower disperse rather than repeat the upper?
10. Where does each major color come from?
11. Can every major new form be traced to the source?
12. Without the middle panel, do the two abstractions still relate?
13. With the middle restored, can a viewer understand their origin?

If these cannot be answered, do not generate.
